=== l6-001 | The lighthouse keeper
focus: intonation
For thirty-one years, Edmund Hale had kept the light on Gull Rock, and in all that time he had never once let it go out. He knew every sound the old tower made: the groan of the iron stairs in a north wind, the tick of the glass as it cooled at dawn, the hollow knock of the sea against the rocks below. Visitors were rare. A supply boat came on the first Monday of each month, weather permitting, and once a summer a party of schoolchildren would climb the hundred and twelve steps and stare out at the grey water as though they expected to see America. Edmund liked the children. He liked their questions, which were always the same. Did he get lonely? What did he eat? Had he ever seen a shipwreck? He answered them patiently, in the slow, careful voice of a man who rarely spoke aloud. Yes, sometimes. Fish, mostly, and tinned peaches. And once, long ago, on a night he still dreamed about. But that story he kept for himself. When the boat left, he would stand on the gallery and wave until it disappeared, and then he would go back inside and polish the lamp.

=== l6-002 | Betty's butter
focus: connected-speech
source: Original, built around the traditional English tongue-twister "Betty Botter" (public domain)
Betty bought a bit of butter, but she said the butter was bitter. If she put this bitter butter in her batter, it would make her batter bitter. So Betty bought a better bit of butter, and the better butter made her batter better. Try saying that three times quickly, and you'll soon discover why tongue-twisters are such good practice. They force your mouth to move between similar sounds at speed, and the moment you lose concentration, the words collapse into a muddle. Here is another to try. Six thick thistle sticks sat in a thin tin box by the thatched shed. Notice how your tongue has to dart forward between your teeth for "thick" and "thin", then pull back quickly for "sat" and "sticks". Now try a sentence that tests your vowels: a proud brown cow wandered round the town, then sat down on a mound and wouldn't be found. Don't worry if you stumble. Start slowly, say each word clearly, and only then build up speed. Within a week or two, you'll find that your speech in everyday conversations becomes clearer and more confident as well.

=== l6-003 | IELTS: a place you like to visit
focus: intonation
I'd like to describe a place that I love to visit, which is a small fishing village on the coast of Cornwall. I first went there with my family when I was about ten years old, and I've been back almost every summer since then. The village sits at the bottom of a steep valley, and the narrow streets lead down towards a tiny harbour full of brightly painted boats. What I like most about it is the atmosphere. In the morning, you can watch the fishermen bringing in their catch, and in the evening, everyone gathers outside the pub to watch the sun go down over the sea. There's no traffic in the centre, so it feels very peaceful compared with the city where I live. I usually spend my days walking along the cliff paths, swimming in the cold water or simply reading on the beach. Of course, it does get crowded in August, and the weather can be unpredictable. But even on a grey, rainy day, I find it a calming place to be. For me, going there is a way of switching off completely from work and the pressures of everyday life.

=== l6-004 | The letter
focus: intonation
The letter arrived on a Tuesday, which Clara would later think was fitting, since nothing important had ever happened to her on a Tuesday before. It lay on the mat among the bills and the leaflets, a thick cream envelope addressed in a hand she did not recognise. She carried it into the kitchen, put the kettle on and sat looking at it for a long time. Her name was written in full, with the middle name she never used, and the postmark was from a town she had never heard of. At last she slid a knife under the flap and drew out two sheets of heavy paper. The first was a short note from a solicitor. The second was a photograph of a house: tall and narrow, with a blue door and a garden full of roses. On the back, in faded pencil, someone had written: "For Clara, when she is ready." The kettle boiled and switched itself off, and she did not notice. She read the note three times, then turned the photograph over and over in her hands, as though the house might tell her who had been waiting all these years for her to be ready.

=== l6-005 | IELTS: technology and work
focus: connected-speech
In many countries, technology has transformed the way people work. Tasks that once took hours, such as writing reports or finding information, can now be completed in minutes. Video calls have made it possible for colleagues in different continents to collaborate as if they were in the same room, and many employees can now work from home for at least part of the week. These changes have clear benefits. Workers save time and money on commuting, and companies can recruit talented people wherever they live. However, there are also significant drawbacks. Some people find it difficult to separate their work from their private life when their office is also their kitchen. Emails and messages arrive at all hours, and there is pressure to reply immediately. In addition, many routine jobs are disappearing as machines and software take them over, and workers in these roles may need to retrain. In my view, the effects of technology on work are largely positive, provided that governments and employers take steps to protect workers' wellbeing. Clear limits on working hours and investment in training would help to ensure that everyone shares in the benefits of these changes.

=== l6-006 | The night train
focus: connected-speech
The night train left the city at a quarter to eleven, and by midnight the lights of the suburbs had given way to a darkness so complete that Tom could see nothing in the window but his own tired face. He had a compartment to himself: a narrow bunk, a folding table and a tiny sink that produced, after some persuasion, a thin stream of lukewarm water. He lay down fully dressed and listened to the rhythm of the wheels. Somewhere along the corridor a baby was crying, and further away a man was laughing at something on the radio. At about two o'clock the train stopped at a station whose name he could not read. A guard walked along the platform with a lamp, calling out softly in a language Tom did not understand. Then a whistle blew, the carriage jolted and the station slid away behind them. He must have slept after that, because the next thing he knew it was morning. Pale sunlight was falling across the blanket, and outside the window there were mountains, enormous and white, standing against a sky of the clearest blue he had ever seen.

=== l6-007 | Red lorry, yellow lorry
focus: connected-speech
source: Original, using the traditional tongue-twister "red lorry, yellow lorry" (public domain)
Actors and broadcasters have always used tongue-twisters to warm up before a performance, and you can use them in exactly the same way. One of the best known in Britain is "red lorry, yellow lorry". Say it once, slowly, and it seems easy. Say it five times, as fast as you can, and the first sounds of "red" and "lorry" begin to tangle together. The secret is to feel the difference in your mouth. For the start of "lorry", the tip of your tongue touches the ridge behind your top teeth. For the start of "red", it curls back slightly and doesn't touch anything at all. Here are a few more to practise. Willy's wheel wobbled while Wendy watched the wet weather. Fresh fried fish, fish fresh fried, fried fish fresh, fish fried fresh. Which wristwatches are Swiss wristwatches? The last one is famously difficult, because it switches between three similar sounds in quick succession. Don't be discouraged if you get them wrong at first. Even professional speakers trip over them. The aim is not perfection but control: the ability to keep each sound clear and distinct, even when you are speaking quickly and naturally in real conversation.

=== l6-008 | IELTS: a person who influenced you
focus: intonation
I'm going to talk about my grandmother, who has probably had more influence on me than anyone else. She grew up on a farm in the countryside and left school at fourteen, but she was one of the wisest people I've ever known. When I was a child, I spent most of my school holidays at her house, and she taught me all sorts of practical things, like how to bake bread, how to grow vegetables and how to mend my own clothes. But the most important thing she taught me was patience. Whenever I got frustrated because something wasn't working, she would say, "Slow down. Do it properly, and do it once." It sounds simple, but I still think of those words whenever I'm under pressure at work or university. She also had an enormous curiosity about the world. Even in her eighties, she was reading books about history and science, and asking me to explain things I'd learned at school. She passed away three years ago, and I miss her a great deal. However, I feel that a lot of her is still with me, in the way I approach problems and in my belief that it's never too late to learn something new.

=== l6-009 | The garden in winter
focus: intonation
In winter the garden belonged to the birds. The flower beds were bare and black, the lawn was stiff with frost, and the old apple tree stood in the corner like a man who had forgotten why he had come outside. But every morning, as soon as it was light, the birds arrived. First came the robin, who regarded the whole garden as his personal property and said so loudly from the top of the fence. Then the blackbirds, turning over the dead leaves with quick, impatient movements, and a pair of blue tits that hung upside down from the feeder like tiny acrobats. Margaret watched them from the kitchen window while she waited for the toast. She had never been especially interested in birds when her husband was alive. It had been his hobby, not hers: his binoculars, his notebooks, his long and to her quite tedious lists. Now she found that she knew most of their names, and that she looked forward to their arrival more than almost anything else in her day. She bought seed in large bags from the shop in the village and filled the feeder every evening, just as he always had, so that nobody would go hungry in the morning.

=== l6-010 | IELTS: cities and the countryside
focus: connected-speech
It is often argued that young people should be encouraged to stay in the countryside rather than move to cities. While I understand the concerns behind this view, I believe that individuals should be free to live wherever they wish. There are certainly problems caused by the movement of young people to urban areas. Rural communities can lose their schools, shops and public transport as their populations age and shrink. Farms may struggle to find workers, and traditional ways of life can disappear. On the other hand, cities offer opportunities that the countryside often cannot match. Universities, hospitals and large employers tend to be concentrated in urban centres, and many careers are simply unavailable elsewhere. It would be unfair to ask a talented young engineer or doctor to give up their ambitions in order to keep a village alive. In my opinion, the best solution is not to discourage people from leaving, but to make rural areas more attractive places to live and work. Faster internet connections, affordable housing and better transport links would allow more people to work remotely from the countryside, and would give those who grew up there a genuine choice about where to build their futures.

=== l6-011 | The storm
focus: intonation
The storm came in from the west just after four o'clock, and by five it was as dark as midnight. Rain hammered on the roof of the farmhouse and ran in rivers down the windows, and every few minutes the whole valley flashed white, followed by a crash of thunder that rattled the plates on the dresser. The children were thrilled. They sat at the kitchen table with their noses pressed against the glass, counting the seconds between the lightning and the thunder to see how far away the storm was. Their mother was less excited. Their father had gone up to the top field after lunch to check on the sheep, and he had not come back. At half past five she lit the lamps and made the children's tea. At six she put on her coat and boots and stood at the back door for a long time, looking up the hill into the rain. Then, just as she had made up her mind to go and look for him, a light appeared at the top of the lane, swinging from side to side, and a familiar voice called out across the yard, asking whether anyone had thought to put the kettle on.

=== l6-012 | Sounds that slide
focus: connected-speech
When British people speak quickly, their words don't stay neatly separated. Sounds join together, change and sometimes disappear entirely, and learners who expect every word to be pronounced clearly are often confused. Take the phrase "what do you want to do". Spoken carefully, it has six distinct words. In rapid speech, it may sound more like a single long word, with the last sound of "what" almost silent and "want to" reduced to something like "wanna". The same thing happens in many common phrases. "Did you" and "would you" can each shrink to a single quick syllable, and "I don't know" may be little more than a rising and falling hum. These changes aren't lazy or careless. They are a natural part of fluent speech, and native speakers use them without thinking. Understanding them will help your listening enormously. You don't need to copy every shortcut in your own speech, but practising a few common ones, such as linking a final consonant to the next vowel in "an apple" or "turn it off", will make you sound smoother and more relaxed. Listen carefully to films, podcasts and conversations, and try to notice where the joins happen.

=== l6-013 | IELTS: describe a celebration
focus: intonation
I'd like to describe my cousin's wedding, which took place last June in a small village in the north of England. It was a fairly traditional wedding, but it was also very relaxed and personal, which is why it was so memorable for me. The ceremony was held in an old stone church in the morning, and afterwards all the guests walked together across a field to a large marquee that had been set up on a farm. The weather was perfect, warm and sunny with a light breeze, so we spent most of the afternoon outside on the grass. The food was all from local producers, and there was a huge cake that my aunt had baked herself. What made the day special, I think, were the speeches. My cousin's father told some very funny stories about her childhood, and her new husband gave a speech that was so sincere that half the guests were in tears. In the evening there was a band, and everyone danced, from the youngest children to my grandfather, who's nearly ninety. I remember thinking that it was the happiest I'd ever seen my whole family together.

=== l6-014 | The old bookshop
focus: connected-speech
The bookshop on Fenwick Lane had been closing down for as long as anyone could remember. A faded sign in the window promised that everything must go, but nothing ever did. Inside, books were piled on every surface: on the shelves, on the floor, on the stairs and even on the single armchair, so that customers had to stand while they read. The owner, Mr Pell, sat behind a counter at the back, half hidden by a wall of encyclopaedias, and rarely looked up. He seemed to regard customers as an interruption. If you asked him for a particular title, he would sigh, disappear among the shelves for ten minutes, and then return with a completely different book, which he would place in your hands without a word. The strange thing was that it was always exactly the right book. People went in looking for a cookery book and came out with a volume of poetry that changed them forever. They went in for a guide to Spain and came out with a novel about Russia that kept them awake all night. Nobody knew how he did it, and nobody dared to ask.

=== l6-015 | IELTS: advertising and children
focus: intonation
Some people believe that advertising aimed at children should be banned. Others argue that such a ban would be unnecessary and unfair. In this essay, I will consider both views before giving my own opinion. Those who support a ban point out that young children cannot easily tell the difference between advertisements and ordinary programmes. They are therefore more likely to believe the claims that advertisers make and to put pressure on their parents to buy products they do not need, such as expensive toys or unhealthy snacks. Over time, this may encourage poor eating habits and an unhealthy focus on possessions. On the other hand, opponents of a ban argue that advertising pays for much of the content that children enjoy, and that removing it could reduce the quality of children's television. They also claim that parents, not governments, should decide what their children watch. In my view, a complete ban would be difficult to enforce, especially online. However, I believe there should be strict rules on advertising unhealthy food to young children, and that schools should teach pupils from an early age to think critically about the messages they see.

=== l6-016 | Peter's pickled peppers
focus: connected-speech
source: Original, built around the traditional English rhyme "Peter Piper" (public domain)
Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers. If Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers, where's the peck of pickled peppers Peter Piper picked? This old rhyme has been used to practise English for generations, and it is still one of the best exercises for the sound at the start of "pen". Hold your hand in front of your mouth as you say it, and you should feel a small puff of air on "Peter", "Piper" and "picked". That puff is called aspiration, and it is one of the things that makes this English sound different from the same letter in many other languages. If you feel nothing, "pen" may sound like "Ben" to a British listener. Now try a newer one that mixes the two sounds. A big black bug bit a big black bear, and the big black bear bled blood. Notice that there is no puff of air for "big" and "black", and that your voice starts before your lips open. Finally, a quick challenge for the end of your practice: please pass the plate of plump purple plums to Paula, but put the pears back in the paper bag. Say each one three times, getting a little faster each time.

=== l6-017 | IELTS: the importance of sleep
focus: intonation
Sleep is one of the most important factors in our health, yet many people in modern societies do not get enough of it. Scientists recommend that adults sleep for between seven and nine hours a night, but surveys suggest that a large proportion of the population regularly sleeps for less than six. There are several reasons for this. Long working hours, stress and caring responsibilities all reduce the time available for rest. In addition, the widespread habit of using phones and tablets in the evening can make it harder to fall asleep, as the light from screens affects the body's natural rhythms. The consequences of poor sleep are serious. In the short term, tired people concentrate less effectively and are more likely to have accidents, both at work and on the roads. In the long term, a lack of sleep has been linked to heart disease, depression and other health problems. There are, however, some simple measures that individuals can take. Going to bed at the same time each night, avoiding caffeine in the afternoon and keeping screens out of the bedroom can all make a significant difference to the quality and length of our sleep.

=== l6-018 | The interview
focus: intonation
Daniel arrived twenty minutes early and spent them walking up and down the street, rehearsing his answers under his breath. He had prepared for every question he could imagine. Why did he want the job? What were his strengths and weaknesses? Where did he see himself in five years? He had answers to all of them, polished and confident and only slightly untrue. At ten o'clock exactly he pushed open the glass door and gave his name to the receptionist, who smiled and asked him to wait. The waiting area was full of plants and very quiet. He sat on the edge of a leather sofa and tried not to look at the other candidate, a young woman in a dark suit who was reading a thick report as though she had nothing at all to worry about. Then a door opened, and a tall, grey-haired man came out and called his name. Daniel stood up too quickly, dropped his folder and watched his carefully printed notes spread across the floor. The man looked at the papers, then at Daniel, and laughed. "Don't worry," he said, kneeling down to help. "That's the most honest thing anyone's done in here all week."

=== l6-019 | A walk along the canal
focus: connected-speech
On Sunday mornings, when the city is still half asleep, I like to walk along the canal. I join the towpath near the station, where the water is dark and still and the old warehouses have been turned into flats with balconies full of plants. For the first mile or so, there are joggers and cyclists and people walking dogs, and everyone nods or says good morning as they pass. Then the buildings thin out, and the canal runs between allotments and back gardens, and finally into open fields. Narrowboats are moored along the bank, painted in reds and greens and decorated with roses and castles, and smoke rises from their chimneys. Sometimes the owners are sitting on deck with a mug of tea, and if I stop to admire a boat, they're usually happy to chat. Herons stand in the shallows like grey statues, and once, just once, I saw the brilliant blue flash of a kingfisher. I usually turn back at the lock, where there's a little cafe that does the best bacon sandwiches I've ever eaten. By the time I get home, the city is awake and busy, but I carry a little of that quiet with me for the rest of the day.

=== l6-020 | IELTS: learning online
focus: intonation
Online learning has grown enormously in recent years, and many people now believe that it will eventually replace traditional classrooms. I partly agree with this view, but I think face-to-face teaching will continue to play an important role. There are clear advantages to studying online. Learners can study at their own pace, at times that suit them, and from anywhere with an internet connection. This is particularly valuable for adults who are working or caring for families, and for people in remote areas who cannot easily travel to a college. Online courses are also often cheaper, and they give students access to teachers and materials from all over the world. However, online learning has limitations. Many students find it difficult to stay motivated without the structure of regular classes and the support of their classmates. Practical subjects, such as nursing, engineering or music, are hard to teach entirely through a screen. Perhaps most importantly, schools and universities are not only places where knowledge is passed on, but also communities where young people develop social skills and make lifelong friends. For these reasons, I believe the future lies in a combination of the two approaches rather than a complete shift to online study.
